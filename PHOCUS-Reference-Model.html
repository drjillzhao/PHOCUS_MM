PHOCUS Maturity Model Reference

Full Reference Model

This table sets out all 20 dimensions across the six PHOCUS categories, and describes what each maturity level — from Observer to Innovator — looks like in practice. Use it alongside your assessment results and action plan to understand what "good" looks like at each stage.

Full Reference Model Overview
L1: Observer L2: Early Adopter L3: Effective Deliverer L4: Leader L5: Innovator
Overview Dimensions Descriptions Level 1 Observer Level 2 Early Adopter Level 3 Effective Deliverer Level 4 Leader Level 5 Innovator
Policy and Funding Policy Organisational policy influence on the ability to deliver PH/ULE social housing. No policy commitment or strategic alignment with PH/ULE goals. Policy shows early interest or is in development. Policy supports PH/ULE delivery. Policy sets high standards with strategic alignment and formal mechanisms for PH/ULE delivery. Policy leads on housing innovation, shaping national agendas, and influencing upstream policy.
Funding Ability to develop and leverage a business model to attract, secure, and influence funding sources beyond conventional routes. No resource or strategy to secure funding for PH/ULE. Organisation plans to recruit resources and seek support for securing suitable funding options. Organisation has a business model and has successfully accessed some non-standard funding. Organisation has experienced in-house bid-writing specialists and regularly secures diverse funding streams. Organisation influences the funder and funding model; advocates for increasing funding for PH/ULE.
Overarching Strategies Delivery strategy Strategy to build a structured approach to effectively implement PH/ULE housing delivery. No structured delivery strategy. Implementation is ad hoc or limited to isolated pilots. Early planning exists but lacks consistency or integration. A structured delivery strategy in place that led to isolated success. Delivery strategy is embedded and reviewed regularly. Processes are replicable and supported by internal capacity. Strategy drives innovation, informs sector best practice, and adapts to emerging challenges.
Quality assurance strategy Structured processes, standards, and verification mechanisms (e.g. certification, on-site inspections, third-party audits, digital monitoring) to ensure PH/ULE projects consistently meet required performance, quality, and compliance outcomes. QA is ad hoc, reactive, and mostly dependent on external requirements (e.g. statutory inspections); little internal consistency. Basic QA processes exist but are inconsistently applied. Limited reporting or feedback loops. QA strategy is structured and applied in some cases. QA is embedded across programmes. Continuous improvement is driven by systematic audits and performance reviews. QA is sector-leading, with predictive quality assurance, third-party certification (e.g. PH, AECB), transparent benchmarking, and sharing of insights to influence industry standards.
Procurement strategy Strategy to establish a strategic, value-driven approach to procurement that supports delivery and aligns with policy objectives. Procurement is basic, or no resources exist to develop a procurement strategy. Some consideration of energy performance and policy alignment in procurement, but not consistently applied. Some support in procurement is available. Established procurement strategy for small-scale PH/ULE delivery. Strategic procurement is embedded. Frameworks and partnerships are used to deliver policy-aligned outcomes at scale. Organisation pioneers innovative procurement models that drive sector transformation and exceed policy expectations.
Organisational Capability Leadership & governance A dedicated team with clear leadership and defined roles. No dedicated leadership or governance structure. Roles and responsibilities are emerging. Some informal tracking of progress. Clear leadership and governance structure in place. Governance is embedded across programmes. Team has authority, resources, and clear accountability for PH/ULE delivery. Leadership champions PH/ULE at a strategic level, shaping organisational culture and influencing sector practices.
Training & skills development Team's knowledge and experience in delivering PH/ULE, and the organisation's capacity to identify, support, and/or co-develop structured training programmes with third parties. No or low level of knowledge of PH/ULE standards. Minimal awareness or investment in PH/ULE-specific training. Basic awareness and informal learning, but limited in scope or not strategically aligned. Team has relevant PH/ULE experience. Structured training programmes are in place and aligned with delivery needs. Knowledgeable and experienced team in PH/ULE delivery. Organisation actively invests in skill-building. Organisation co-develops training programmes with third-party partners (e.g. PHT, AECB) to lead sector-wide skills development.
Organisational culture A culture of sustainability with evidence built into organisational KPIs and value/behaviour frameworks. No visible culture of sustainability. Emerging awareness of sustainability values. Sustainability thinking is reflected in some KPIs and staff behaviours. Strong sustainability culture embedded in behaviour frameworks. Organisation is a catalyst for sector-wide sustainability cultural change.
Cost-benefit analysis capability Ability to conduct cost-benefit and long-term viability assessments (30–40 years), and build effective business cases to support investment decisions. No resource or ability to conduct long-term cost-benefit analysis. Basic cost-benefit assessments are conducted, but long-term viability is not fully considered. Comprehensive cost-benefit analysis is conducted in isolated cases and supports strategic investment decisions. Long-term cost-benefit modelling is embedded in planning; informs funding and delivery strategies. Organisation leads in cost-benefit modelling innovation, demonstrating sector-wide value through robust long-term viability assessments.
Stakeholder engagement Ability to ensure strategic and operational backing from stakeholders to enable implementation, sustain momentum, and influence wider sector adoption of PH/ULE practices. Limited or reactive engagement. Initial efforts to engage stakeholders. Stakeholder engagement is structured and ongoing in some cases. Organisation builds strong partnerships and co-ownership of delivery. Stakeholders actively contribute to planning and implementation. Organisation co-creates PH/ULE strategies with stakeholders, building alliances that influence wider sector adoption and policy.
Process Maturity Process documentation Develop and maintain clear, standardised processes tailored to PH/ULE requirements; ensure all project phases (design, procurement, construction, commissioning) are mapped and aligned with PH/ULE principles (RIBA overlay). Processes are undocumented or generic; no PH/ULE requirements. Some documentation exists; PH/ULE requirements are partially considered. Standardised processes are in place across key phases with PH/ULE principles embedded. Processes are well-defined, aligned with frameworks like the RIBA overlay, and consistently applied. Organisation leads in developing PH/ULE-specific process documentation, continuously optimised and shared across the sector as a benchmark.
Project management Ensure robust Time, Cost, and Scope (TCS) management specifically for PH/ULE projects. TCS management is informal or reactive. Basic project planning exists; limited control over TCS. Structured project management with defined TCS controls and some contractual clarity. Robust TCS management and transparent contracts; PH/ULE requirements are well integrated. Advanced project controls and agile delivery models; continuously improves TCS performance and shares learning across the sector.
Risk management The organisation's willingness and capacity to identify, assess, and manage risks specific to PH/ULE delivery (e.g. design detailing, cost overruns, supply chain gaps), and implement structured mitigation strategies. Risk-averse; no formal risk approach; risks are not identified or managed. Some awareness of PH/ULE risks; limited clarity on acceptable risk levels or mitigation strategies. Risks are identified and monitored; mitigation strategies are in place and aligned with project goals. Risk appetite is strategic and forward-looking; proactive identification and structured mitigation of PH/ULE-specific risks. Risk is controlled and reduced through lessons learned; influences industry standards via scenario planning and shared learning.
Post occupancy management Structured plans for Soft Landings and Landlord/Tenant Liaison to ensure community engagement, user education, and feedback mechanisms for tenant experience and satisfaction. No formal post-occupancy strategy. Limited tenant engagement or feedback collection. Basic handover support; limited tenant education or feedback collection. Structured Soft Landings plan; some tenant liaison and feedback processes in place. Clear engagement strategy (Soft Landings, Landlord & Tenant Liaison Plans); proactive education and feedback used to improve performance. Fully integrated post-occupancy framework; sector-leading engagement and feedback systems inform future design and delivery.
Monitoring, feedback & iteration Enable continuous improvement through feedback loops, including post-occupancy evaluation (POE) and real-time monitoring (e.g. sensors for air quality, humidity, temperature). No structured monitoring or POE tools. Performance data is not collected or used. Some monitoring tools used in isolated projects. Data collection is limited and not systematically analysed. POE and real-time monitoring systems implemented across projects. Data is stored and used to inform improvements. Organisation uses centralised digital systems and pattern books; insights drive design refinement and operational improvements. Organisation leads in data-driven POE with real-time analytics; developed a central digital database for building performance data.
Supply Chain Readiness Supply chain engagement Ability to engage and influence suppliers' willingness, commitment, investment, and participation in delivering PH/ULE projects. Not engaged with supply chain; minimal influence on their willingness to deliver PH/ULE projects. Some dialogue with suppliers; limited influence on their understanding or commitment. Actively engages suppliers with PH/ULE requirements; some suppliers show commitment and investment under influence. Builds strategic relationships; suppliers are aligned and invested in PH/ULE delivery. Suppliers co-drive innovation and promote PH/ULE adoption across projects and the wider market.
Supplier training, certification & skills Level of expertise, training, and technical ability within the supply chain to deliver high-performance PH/ULE projects, including use of PHPP or other energy modelling software. No support or guidance provided; suppliers lack relevant skills and tools. Some suppliers have basic awareness; limited technical ability, guidance, and awareness of tools like PHPP. Suppliers have relevant experience; training and use of PHPP or similar tools are supported. Actively co-develops training programmes with suppliers; promotes certification and technical upskilling. Organisation leads supply chain transformation, working with certifiers and training bodies (e.g. Passivhaus tradesperson).
Joint delivery & innovation Suppliers' capacity to innovate in advanced methods, materials, and prefabrication to improve cost, speed, and quality, and how the client stimulates or incentivises this. No innovation encouraged; conventional approaches dominate. Some interest in innovation; limited access to advanced components or prefabrication. Organisation supports suppliers in adopting improved methods and materials; some innovation evident. Actively incentivises innovation in prefabrication, components, and delivery efficiency; suppliers engaged in co-development. Drives sector-wide innovation; co-creates new solutions and prototypes (e.g. pattern books) with suppliers.
Integration and Scalability Supply chain integration Align procurement, supply chain, and project delivery through coordinated frameworks. Foster early contractor involvement (ECI) to reduce risk and increase build quality. Minimal coordination; procurement and supply chain operate in isolation with little early involvement. Some coordination exists; early contractor involvement (ECI) is rare or informal. Procurement and delivery are partially aligned; ECI used in some projects to improve outcomes. Coordinated frameworks in place; strong partnerships and consistent ECI reduce risk and improve quality. Organisation leads in integrated delivery, using standardised tools (e.g. PH Responsibility Matrix) and collaborative delivery models.
Scalability & market growth Ability to scale from delivering a pilot project to multiple projects consistently over time, maintaining quality, timeliness, and cost-effectiveness. Not delivered any PH/ULE project. Delivers isolated projects with no clear strategy for scaling; processes are informal and reactive. Begins to replicate pilot success. Basic systems and planning are emerging, but scalability is limited. Capable of delivering multiple projects reliably; quality, timeliness, and cost-effectiveness are maintained. Expanding capacity systematically, maintaining high standards, and setting benchmarks for sector-wide scalability.
